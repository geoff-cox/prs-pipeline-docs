# PROTEUS — Orientation

**What we're doing, and where you fit.** Students answer short "explain why" questions in the *Understanding Linear Algebra* (ULA) textbook, and their written justifications show *how they decide an answer is settled* — the criterion or rule they lean on. Our project builds a tool that assists the human coding of that reasoning, but the tool is only publishable if it agrees with careful human coders. **You two are those coders.** The set of responses you code independently, disagree on, and then adjudicate is the gold standard the whole project is measured against — it is the empirical core of the paper, not support work. You'll work entirely in shared spreadsheets and short memos; you will never write, run, or edit code, and never edit the repository.

**The one thing that matters most.** Given a real student response, you can decompose it into **episodes** — the *criterion* the student cites, the *inferential link*, and the *judgment* they draw — and record that decomposition **consistently**, marking when a piece is missing and when you're unsure. Everything else in the project either feeds this skill or sits downstream of it. Get comfortable with this and you can do the work.

**What to read now — just one thing.** Read the **Coder's Handbook, Part I** (`01-coders-handbook.md`) closely; it is your instrument. Keep the project **glossary** (`docs/source/sa/bookends/glossary.ptx`) bookmarked to look up terms — don't read it end to end. That's it for now. The chapters describing the *tool itself* (the roadmap and pipeline demos) come later, when you start reviewing the tool's output. You don't need them to code, and reading them now can quietly nudge you into coding the way the tool does — which is exactly what we need you *not* to do, since your independence is what makes the comparison meaningful.

**Don't read yet — this protects a one-time measurement.** Your first coding task after the warm-up is a *blind pass*: you code real responses *before* seeing the project's codebook, so we can test whether its categories are ones fresh readers discover on their own. That test works only once. Until [PI NAME] clears you, do **not** open: the sealed companion (`02-codebook-companion-SEALED.md`); the codebook CSV; or the vocabulary/codebook sections of `ch-technical-notes.ptx`. Catching a stray example criterion in passing is fine — it's the *full lists* of codes and terms we're protecting. Open one by accident? Just tell [PI NAME]; honesty beats pretending.

**Ground rules.**
- **Independence.** During any "independent" pass, don't compare responses or codes with each other. The disagreement between you is something we measure and publish; comparing early destroys it.
- **Ask about rules anytime.** Unsure how a *rule* applies? Ask [PI NAME] (not each other). Answers are shared with both of you and logged — every clarification is a candidate handbook revision.
- **Record uncertainty.** A low-confidence code with a good note beats a silently forced one.
- **Verbatim means copy-paste** in any "verbatim" column — don't paraphrase or fix spelling.
- **Nothing is graded.** These are anonymized research data; you're describing reasoning, not judging students.

**Data & logistics.** Response data reaches you only through [SHARED FOLDER LINK] — never in the repo, in email, or in any AI/chat tool. Once a week, send [PI NAME] five quick bullets: what you did · roughly how long · what confused you · what's next · anything blocking you (two minutes; "time spent" is one of the things we measure). And since the Handbook is the one document you're reading, **flag anything in it that's unclear** — you're its first outside readers.
